Midterm Study Guide

Our midterm exam will take place during lecture on Thursday October 8. Here’s what you should know about it:

  • What’s the format? It’s a traditional pencil-and-paper exam. The questions will be a mix of multiple choice and free response;
  • How long will it take? You have 75 minutes to complete the exam, but on average the exam is not intended to take the full 75 minutes;
  • What resources are allowed? You are only allowed both sides of a single 8.5” x 11” sheet of notes created by you and you only. I do not care how you create the note sheet;
  • Will we be writing code on the exam? No. However, you may be asked to analyze and interpret the output of some code, and so being able to read the code that generated the output could be helpful;
  • What skills will be emphasized? I care most about your visual intuition and conceptual understanding, so you should sweep through all of the lectures, find every picture we ever dissected, and make sure you understand every detail of it;
  • What topics will be covered? Everything from Lecture 2 on 9/1 through Lecture 11 on 10/6 is fair game. So that’s exploratory data analysis (visualization and summarization) and regression modeling (both linear and logistic);
  • Should I study the textbook, the lecture videos, or the tutorials? Maybe if you have time, but you should not prioritize them. In approximate order of importance, you should prioritize studying this guide, the lecture notes, the solutions to the in-class demos (so-called AEs), the homeworks, and the labs. If you have more time to study after all of that, then the first thing I would suggest is sampling some of the odd-numbered exercises at the end of Ch. 1 - 10 in IMS. The answers to these exercises are in the book, and they do not involve code: just concepts, interpretation, etc;
    • Note: if you do dip into the textbook, videos, or tutorials, and you encounter a topic that never appeared in any of the lectures, labs, or homeworks, then you are not responsible for it;
  • One more thing: don’t waste your time studying the details of any specific applications or datasets we’ve seen (eg. penguins, Bechdel, gerrymandering, diamonds, etc). Real datasets will of course be discussed on the exam, but everything you need to know about them will be introduced from scratch within the exam as if you had never seen them before.

Below are a bunch of practice problems to get your wheels spinning. Solutions will be posted on Monday October 5.

Try this

Attempt all of the problems below without resources. Make note of your pain points and blind spots along the way. That might give you an idea of the stuff you would benefit from if you had it on a cheat sheet.

Concepts and main ideas

Question 1

Can you answer each of these in one or two sentences?

  1. If I have a column of numbers, what is the definition of the 38% quantile?
    • Could you sketch a cartoon of what this looks like?
    • Is the 38% quantile smaller than, bigger than, or equal to the median, or do you not have enough information to say?
  2. What does the correlation coefficient between two numerical variables measure?
  3. What is the difference between \(y=\beta_0+\beta_1 x + \varepsilon\) and \(\hat{y}=b_0+b_1 x\)?
  4. When we plot a line of best fit for a numerical predictor and numerical response, what does “best” mean?
  5. Consider this bit of toy code: linear_reg() |> fit(_BLANK_, df)? What goes in the blank? What purpose does that argument serve, and how do you use it?
  6. Under what circumstances is the intercept in a regression model a meaningful quantity?
  7. If you run a “simple” linear regression model with a numerical response and a categorical predictor with three levels, what does the computer actually do to estimate that model?
  8. Let \(\hat{y}^{(\text{old})}\) be the prediction at \(x\) of a simple linear regression, and let \(\hat{y}^{(\text{new})}\) be the prediction at \(x+1\) of the same regression. How are \(\hat{y}^{(\text{old})}\) and \(\hat{y}^{(\text{new})}\) related? In other words, how does the prediction of the simple linear regression model change when you change the input from \(x\) to \(x+1\)?
  9. What does the \(R^2\) of a linear regression measure?
  10. Why is adjusted \(R^2\) a better model selection criterion than ordinary, unadjusted \(R^2\)?
  11. Let \(\hat{o}^{(\text{old})}\) be the estimated odds at \(x\) from a simple logistic regression model, and let \(\hat{o}^{(\text{new})}\) be the estimated odds at \(x+1\) of the same regression. How are \(\hat{o}^{(\text{old})}\) and \(\hat{o}^{(\text{new})}\) related? In other words, how does the prediction of the simple logistic regression model change when you change the input from \(x\) to \(x+1\)?
  12. What is the main lesson of Anscombe’s Quartet?
